Most of a content pipeline benefits from automation — design consistency, distribution, even a first draft of commentary on something timely. This isn't a piece arguing against any of that. It's about the specific, narrower set of things that shouldn't be automated at all, or shouldn't be automated *past a human check*, and why the cost of getting this wrong is worse than the time saved.

## Replies and community interaction

Automated replies are detectable, and audiences treat a detected automated reply as a small betrayal of the actual relationship they thought they had with an account. This is different from automating a scheduled post — a reply implies a person is actually there, present, responding to *you specifically*, and automation breaks that implication the moment it's noticed.

## Anything claiming a personal experience or opinion you don't actually hold

An AI-drafted reaction to news, reviewed and approved by you before it publishes, is your actual opinion wearing AI-assisted phrasing — that's fine. An automation that publishes a claimed personal experience or strong opinion *without* your review crosses into publishing something under your name that you never actually said. The line isn't "did AI help write it," it's "did a human confirm this is actually what we mean."

## Crises, apologies, and anything unfolding in real time

A situation serious enough to need an apology or a real-time crisis response needs a human making live judgment calls, not a workflow — even one with a fast poll and a tight review step. The stakes are too asymmetric: automation's value is consistency and speed on routine decisions, and a crisis is neither routine nor safely delegable to "usually gets it right."

## The final publish step, until a workflow has earned trust

This is less an absolute rule than a sequencing one — every workflow in this whole series has said the same thing: keep the Trigger on manual, or otherwise review before publish, until you've watched a workflow handle real, varied input correctly more than once. This isn't "never automate publishing" — it's "earn it first," and the [pre-launch checklist](/blog/automation-pre-launch-checklist) exists specifically to make that earning process concrete rather than vague.

## What's actually fine to fully automate

Worth being equally clear about the other side: design consistency (templates, brand kit), distribution mechanics (cross-posting, scheduling), drafting (with review), and recurring low-stakes formats (weekly recaps, countdowns, price snapshots) are all things this whole blog has argued *should* be automated, often all the way to a timer, because the cost of an occasional imperfect run is genuinely low and the time saved is genuinely large.

## The actual test

Before automating any specific piece of a content pipeline past a human check, ask: if this specific run goes wrong, how bad is it, and how visible? A slightly awkward recap post is low-cost and barely noticed. A tone-deaf reply, a claimed experience that isn't true, or a crisis response drafted by a workflow are high-cost and highly visible the moment they go wrong. Automate confidently where the answer is "low stakes, quickly fixed" — keep a human directly in the loop everywhere the answer isn't.
