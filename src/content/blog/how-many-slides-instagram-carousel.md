Instagram allows up to 20 slides per carousel, and almost no carousel should use anywhere near that many. The gap between the platform's technical maximum and what actually performs is the whole story here — and the right number for any given post isn't a fixed rule, it's a match between slide count and how much the content genuinely has to say.

## The ceiling and the sweet spot are different numbers

**20** is Instagram's hard limit. **6–10** is where most well-performing carousels land, and for a specific mechanical reason: every additional slide is another chance for a viewer to stop swiping before the end, and completion rate — whether someone reaches the final slide — is one of the signals the algorithm weighs. A 20-slide carousel might earn a save from the fraction of viewers who finish it, but a much smaller fraction finishes it at all compared to an 8-slide version of the same idea.

That tension is the whole design problem: more slides means more surface area to earn something (a save, a follow, a link click) from anyone who does complete the swipe, but a lower and lower share of viewers actually get there as length grows. Six to ten slides is where that trade-off tends to land in favor of the creator — long enough to develop an idea properly, short enough that most people who start actually finish.

## The number should come from the content, not a rule of thumb

The most common mistake in both directions is treating slide count as a decision made before the content exists, rather than a consequence of it:

- **Padding.** A carousel with three genuine points stretched to ten slides — restating the same idea with different wording, adding a slide that's really just decoration — tests as long but reads as thin. Viewers notice the padding faster than they notice the slide count; the carousel loses swipe momentum exactly where it's stretched thinnest.
- **Cramming.** The opposite failure: a genuinely ten-point idea forced into five slides, each one carrying two ideas at once. This defeats the entire purpose of the format — [one idea per slide](/blog/carousel-hook-slide-design) is what makes a carousel easy to swipe through; two ideas per slide turns each swipe into a re-read instead of a quick take.

The fix in both directions is the same: outline the actual content first — how many genuinely distinct points does this idea have? — and let that number, not a target slide count, determine the length. Most outlines that get this treatment honestly land somewhere in 6–10 anyway, which is exactly why that range is the norm rather than an arbitrary rule.

## When going outside 6–10 is the right call

A tightly scoped single insight (one myth, one before/after, one stat with context) can genuinely be 4–5 slides and still feel complete — forcing it to six pads it. A dense how-to with real sequential steps can legitimately run 12–14 slides if every one of them earns its place and the hook slide sets expectations honestly (so a viewer who commits to swiping isn't surprised by the length). What doesn't work is hitting either extreme by accident — a five-slide carousel because the last three ideas got cut for time, or a fourteen-slide one because nothing got edited down.

## Test it rather than guess it

Slide count is one of the more measurable design decisions in your content system, because completion/swipe-through rate is a real, trackable number per post — see [designing for swipe-through rate](/blog/design-carousels-for-swipe-through-rate) for what to actually watch, and [A/B testing templates](/blog/ab-testing-templates-automation) for comparing a longer and shorter treatment of similar content directly rather than debating it in the abstract. Over a handful of posts, your own audience's completion data is a far better guide to your ideal length than any general benchmark, including this one.
