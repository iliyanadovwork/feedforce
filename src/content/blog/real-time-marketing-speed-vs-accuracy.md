Real-time marketing has a famous highlight reel — the ad that nailed a live event, the brand that posted the perfect line within minutes of something breaking — and a much quieter blooper reel of brands that reacted to something false, tasteless, or already over. Both outcomes come from the same instinct: move fast. The actual lesson from comparing the wins to the misfires isn't "move slower" — it's that the time saved by moving fast should go entirely into watching and drafting, never into skipping the one check that catches a bad call.

## Where the time actually goes in a manual process

A typical manual real-time marketing attempt: someone notices a story, flags it, a few people discuss whether to respond, someone drafts a line, it gets approved, it goes out. Most of that time — noticing, discussing whether it's worth it, drafting a first pass — is genuinely wasted motion, not careful thought. The actual judgment call, "should we say this, publicly, right now," takes seconds once a good draft exists in front of you.

## What automation should — and shouldn't — compress

Automating the watching and drafting stages (see [always-on news monitoring](/blog/always-on-news-monitoring-automation) and [automating newsjacking](/blog/automate-newsjacking)) removes exactly the wasted motion: a relevant story is flagged the moment it clears your filter, a draft in your voice exists within seconds of that. What shouldn't compress is the actual decision — the same few seconds of "is this actually good, actually true, actually us" that mattered in the manual process still matter here. Automation that removes *that* step isn't making real-time marketing faster; it's making it reckless, and the failure mode looks identical to the misfires that happen without any automation at all.

## The three checks that catch almost every misfire

Looking at real-time marketing failures across the ones that get remembered, nearly all trace to one of three missed checks — all cheap, all fast, none worth automating away:

1. **Is this actually true?** Breaking stories get revised or retracted constantly in their first hour. A reaction to the initial, wrong version ages badly fast.
2. **Is this actually in good taste?** A witty take on a trending topic can land very differently once more details emerge — a joke about an event that turns out to involve real harm is the classic version of this.
3. **Is this actually still relevant?** The gap between "drafted" and "published" is sometimes long enough that a story has already moved past the angle you drafted against.

All three take a human a few seconds to check against a finished draft. None of them take a human hours to check — which is exactly why compressing the drafting time (via automation) while keeping this check intact is a pure win, not a trade-off at all.

## Reframing the trade-off

The common framing — "fast and risky" versus "slow and safe" — isn't quite right. The real choice is between a **slow, wasteful process** (most of the delay is administrative, not judgment) and a **fast, disciplined one** (the delay that remains is entirely the judgment call, compressed to its actual minimum). Automation is what makes the second option available; it was never available manually, because manually, the wasted motion and the judgment call were tangled together in the same slow process.

## What this looks like day to day

An automated watch-and-draft pipeline (see [always-on news monitoring](/blog/always-on-news-monitoring-automation)) surfaces a genuinely relevant, recent story with a drafted, on-brand reaction already written. You read it, and in the time it takes to read a sentence, you know whether it's true, in taste, and still timely. That's the entire real-time marketing workflow, done right — fast where speed is free, careful where carelessness is expensive, and never confusing the two.
