A carousel with nine excellent slides and one weak opening slide performs like a carousel with one slide — because nobody swipes past a hook that doesn't work, and the other nine never get seen. Slide one isn't equal to the rest; it's the gatekeeper for all of them.

## What a hook slide's one job actually is

Not to summarize the carousel, not to introduce the topic politely — to create one specific reason to swipe to slide two. A hook slide that reads like a title page ("5 Tips for Better Mornings") is dramatically weaker than one that makes a claim or asks a question specific enough to create genuine curiosity ("The one morning habit that's actually wasting your time").

## Why summarizing the content is the most common mistake

The instinct is to use slide one to explain what's coming — but a slide that already tells the reader the gist removes their reason to keep swiping. The hook should create a gap between what's promised and what's delivered, not close it early. Save the actual content for the slides earning the swipe, not the one asking for it.

## Type size and hierarchy matter more here than anywhere else

Because a hook slide is evaluated in a fraction of a second during a scroll, its text needs to be readable at a glance — larger type than you'd use anywhere else in the carousel, one dominant line rather than several competing ones. If a viewer has to slow down and actually read carefully to get the hook, it's already lost most of its effectiveness against a feed that rewards instant legibility.

## Branding without competing for attention

Your logo and brand colors should be present (consistency matters — see [what belongs in a brand kit](/blog/social-media-brand-kit-guide)) but sized so they don't compete with the hook text itself. A hook slide is not the place for a prominent logo placement; it's the place for the one line of text doing all the work.

## Testing hook slides specifically

Because the hook slide's entire job is measurable (did people swipe?), it's one of the more testable elements in your whole content system — see [A/B testing templates](/blog/ab-testing-templates-automation) for running a genuine comparison between two hook approaches on the same underlying content, and [swipe-through rate as the actual metric to design for](/blog/design-carousels-for-swipe-through-rate) rather than judging a hook by how it looks in isolation.

## Building a repeatable hook formula

Rather than reinventing the hook from scratch each time, a small set of proven patterns — a specific claim, a surprising number, a direct question, a "most people get this wrong" framing — can become a repeatable formula you rotate through, adapted to each carousel's actual content. This is exactly the kind of decision worth baking into a [template](/blog/how-template-placeholders-work) rather than re-deciding fresh every single carousel.
