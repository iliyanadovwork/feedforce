The pixel dimensions of a cross-posted video are the part everyone gets right, because they're the part with a single correct number. The part that actually reveals a cross-post — and that most guides skip — is everything downstream of the visual: how the caption reads, how hashtags are used, whether the audio survives the move, and whether the post feels like it was made *for* the platform it landed on or just *dropped into* it.

## Caption tone and length aren't interchangeable

Every platform has trained its audience to expect a certain caption shape, and violating it is the fastest way to look like a copy-paste job:

- **TikTok** rewards a natural-language, searchable sentence — TikTok's own search behaves closer to a search engine than a social feed, so a caption phrased as a real query ("how to fix this in 30 seconds") outperforms a punchy one-liner.
- **Instagram** wants something tighter, often followed by a small, relevant hashtag cluster (3–5, not 20).
- **X** is text-first — the caption *is* the post, with media attached underneath, so it needs to carry the point on its own rather than gesture at a video for context.
- **LinkedIn** expects professional framing: what this means for the reader's work, not just what happened. The same clip needs a "why this matters" sentence added that the other platforms don't need.
- **YouTube (including Shorts)** rewards a more descriptive, keyword-forward line closer to a title than a caption — YouTube's discovery still runs heavily on search and titles.

Writing one caption and pasting it everywhere skips all five of these norms at once — it's the single most common tell that a post was cross-posted rather than made for the platform.

## Hashtag culture is not the same instinct everywhere

TikTok and Instagram both use hashtags, which makes it tempting to treat them as interchangeable — they're not. TikTok hashtags function closer to search terms and broad content categories; a video with #fyp and a handful of topic tags is normal. Instagram hashtags work better narrower and more specific, and a wall of 30 tags reads as spam rather than discovery intent. LinkedIn and X use hashtags sparingly if at all — one or two, if any, tied to something genuinely being discussed. Importing a TikTok-style hashtag block into a LinkedIn post is one of the more obvious cross-posting tells, because almost nobody native to LinkedIn posts that way.

## Audio licensing doesn't travel with the file

A song cleared for use on TikTok under TikTok's commercial music library is not automatically cleared for Instagram, and vice versa — the license lives with the platform, not the video file. A video whose whole appeal depends on a specific trending sound can get muted, or the whole post can be limited in reach, on a platform where that license doesn't carry over. When audio matters to the content, check the destination platform's own sound library for the same or a similar track rather than assuming the original audio survives the move intact.

## Native-feeling vs. obviously recycled

None of this means every cross-post needs a from-scratch redesign — the visual asset can be, and usually should be, identical across platforms (see [posting to all platforms at once](/blog/post-to-all-social-media-at-once) for the one-master approach). What separates a native-feeling repost from an obvious copy-paste is entirely in this layer: caption, hashtags, and audio adapted to where the post actually lives. Get the visual right once, then spend the extra two minutes per platform on this layer — it's the difference viewers and algorithms both notice, even when they can't say exactly why one version feels more "for them" than another.
