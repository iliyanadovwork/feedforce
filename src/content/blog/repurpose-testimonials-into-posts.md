Testimonial posts have a trust problem before they even get designed: readers assume marketing has already sanded down anything a real customer said. The fix isn't a better quote — it's leaving the quote alone and being disciplined about where it came from and how it's attributed. Here's the actual process, from finding raw material to keeping a recognizable format week after week.

## Where testimonials actually come from

Formal reviews (G2, Google, App Store) are the obvious source, but they're often the weakest, because they were written knowing they'd be public — the language skews toward generic praise. Better sources, in rough order of how convincing they read once quoted:

- **Support tickets and DM replies** — a customer describing a specific problem and what fixed it, written with zero awareness it might be quoted later. This is the richest source by far.
- **Case study interviews** — longer, structured, but often contain one throwaway line that's better than anything in the prepared answers.
- **Unprompted social mentions** — someone tagging you or mentioning you without being asked. Rare, but the strongest trust signal when it happens.
- **Formal reviews** — useful for star ratings and volume, weaker for standalone quotable lines.

Go looking in support and DMs specifically, not just your review inbox. That's where the sentences that don't sound like marketing actually live.

## Turning a raw line into a quote card without flattening it

The instinct when designing a testimonial post is to clean up the quote — fix the grammar, trim the rambling, make it sound polished. Resist most of that instinct. A testimonial that reads slightly rough, with a specific detail intact ("saved me about 3 hours every Monday," not "saves so much time"), is more believable than a smoothed-over version, because smoothing is exactly what fabricated marketing copy looks like.

What you should edit:

- **Cut, don't rewrite.** Shorten a long message by removing sentences, not by rephrasing the ones you keep.
- **Keep the specific detail.** A number, a named feature, a concrete moment — these are what make it read as real. Generic enthusiasm ("love this product!") is the part safe to cut if you need length.
- **Attribute precisely.** First name and role or company if you have permission for both; first name only if that's what you have consent for. Never upgrade "a customer" into a fabricated name or invent a role they didn't confirm.

## Permission and attribution, without the guesswork

The rule that avoids most mistakes: **the more identifiable the attribution, the more explicit the permission needs to be.** A quote with no name attached is the lowest-risk version and needs the least permission (though still ask if it came from a private channel). A quote with a name, company, and photo needs clear, specific consent for exactly that combination — someone agreeing to a case study interview hasn't automatically agreed to their headshot on a Reels quote card. When in doubt, send a one-line message asking to use their exact words publicly with their name; most customers say yes, and the ones who don't have just saved you an awkward takedown request later.

## Making it a recognizable format

A testimonial post should be identifiable as "one of the testimonial posts" before anyone reads the words — same template, same logo placement, same color treatment every time, with only the quote and name changing. That consistency is what turns a single social-proof post into a *series* readers start to recognize and trust cumulatively, the same way a recurring format works for any content type.
