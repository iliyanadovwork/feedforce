On most platforms carousels are one format among many. On LinkedIn they're an anomaly: **document posts routinely earn 2–3x the reach of anything else you can publish**, because every swipe is dwell time and dwell time is what LinkedIn's algorithm pays for. They're also the most awkwardly implemented format on any major network — you're uploading a PDF, of all things. This guide covers the mechanics, the numbers, and a production system that makes them repeatable.

## LinkedIn carousels are PDFs (yes, really)

There's no "add more images" button like Instagram. A LinkedIn carousel is a **document post**: you design your slides, export them as a single multi-page PDF, and upload it via the document icon in the post composer. LinkedIn renders each page as a swipeable card in the feed.

Practical consequences of the PDF mechanism:

- **Fonts must be embedded or outlined** — a PDF referencing a font the renderer lacks falls back to something ugly. Export from a design tool, never "print to PDF" from a browser.
- **No animation, no video pages.** Static pages only.
- **Text stays selectable** in the expanded view — a small accessibility and SEO bonus Instagram carousels don't have.
- **The first page is your thumbnail.** It renders small in-feed, so it needs poster-level type sizes.

## Dimensions and structure

**Size:** 1080×1350 (4:5) portrait for maximum feed height, or 1080×1080 if you want extra safety on desktop. Same rule as [Instagram carousels](/blog/instagram-carousel-size-guide): pick one ratio and keep every page on it.

**Length:** 8–12 pages. The structure that consistently works:

1. **Page 1 — the hook.** One bold claim or question in huge type. Its only job: the first swipe.
2. **Page 2 — the stakes.** Why this matters to the reader, one sentence.
3. **Pages 3–10 — one point per page.** A heading and 1–3 short lines each. If a page needs a paragraph, it's two pages.
4. **Last page — the CTA.** On LinkedIn, "Follow for more" plus a comment prompt outperforms external links (which the algorithm quietly dampens).

**Type sizes:** headlines 60–90pt equivalents, body text never below ~30pt at 1080px width. LinkedIn's in-feed preview is small and the audience skims on laptops in meetings.

## Why the reach premium exists (and how long it'll last)

LinkedIn's feed optimizes for time-on-post more nakedly than any other network. A ten-page carousel that takes 40 seconds to swipe generates an order of magnitude more dwell than a text post read in four. Multiple large-scale analyses of LinkedIn engagement data have measured document posts at roughly two to three times median reach — the gap has narrowed since the 2022–2023 peak as the format saturated, but it remains the strongest organic format on the platform.

The honest caveat: reach premiums decay as formats saturate. The durable advantage isn't the format itself — it's having a **system that produces good ones cheaply**, so you're consistently present with the format while competitors treat each carousel as a design project.

## The production system

A LinkedIn carousel has 8–12 pages of designed content, which is exactly why most companies post one, exhaust themselves, and stop. The fix is the same as every content-scaling fix — templates plus pipeline:

1. **Define 2–3 carousel formats as templates** — "listicle," "myth vs fact," "how we did X" — with your brand kit (fonts, colors, logo placement) locked in. In [FeedForce](/) this is the template builder; a new carousel becomes filling text slots, not opening a blank canvas.
2. **Write all pages as plain text first.** The carousel is an essay with one idea per paragraph; write it as bullets before touching design.
3. **Batch-produce weekly** — two carousels in one sitting beats two separate design sessions.
4. **Repurpose the same set to Instagram** (as native images there, not PDF) — one design, two platforms; see [posting everywhere at once](/blog/post-to-all-social-media-at-once).

The companies winning LinkedIn right now aren't better designers. They've just made carousel #40 cost a tenth of what carousel #1 cost — and the algorithm rewards whoever shows up every week with dwell-worthy pages.
